The problem

Melting glaciers leave lakes held back by loose walls of rock and rubble. Sometimes a wall gives way and the whole lake empties in hours, destroying everything downstream. South Lhonak Lake in Sikkim did exactly this on 4 October 2023. The warning signs sat in free satellite pictures for years — but nobody was watching them.

My job on the team is the modelling: turn those satellite measurements into a risk score for each lake, every 10 days.

The idea — flip the problem

You can't teach a computer to recognise a disaster from one example. So instead of learning what a bursting lake looks like, it learns what each lake looks like when it's calm — and there are years of calm data, free. Then it just measures how far today sits from that lake's own normal.

The one real disaster is never used for teaching. It's saved as the final exam — we check whether the system would have caught it, using only data from before it happened.

How it works, in six steps

1

Strip out the seasons

Every glacial lake grows in summer. The system learns each lake's yearly rhythm and removes it, so summer isn't flagged as danger.

2

Give each lake its own model

A big lake and a small lake are never compared directly. Each is judged only against its own history.

3

Spot the odd windows

Isolation Forest: it makes random cuts to separate a data point from the crowd. A normal week takes many cuts; an odd week takes very few. Few cuts = suspicious.

4

Combine into one risk score

The signals — lake size, radar texture, warmth, glacier speed — are merged into a single number from 0 to 1, weighting the ones that matter most.

5

Name the reason

It doesn't just say "risk is high." It says which signals drove it — "lake area · thermal" — so an officer understands why.

6

Admit when it's blind

If cloud blocked the view it says "cannot assess" — never "safe." Saying "I don't know" and "it's fine" are completely different things.

The final exam — Sikkim 2023

Trained only on data from before 4 October 2023, would the system have flagged South Lhonak before it burst? It did — its risk climbed through September into the concern band as the burst approached, with lake area entering the reason.

We removed each signal and checked whether the warning got weaker. Two did the work; the rest barely helped in this data — an honest, useful finding for the report.
